A Smart & Brown Model L, serial number S10312, built in 1961 and sold
through Buck & Hickman. Its own headstock is of the type the catalogue calls
the PDH, and it carries a second headstock, a PRH, from another machine. How
that was worked out, and what I got wrong on the way, is in the log entries
[Which Model L](../log/2026-10-12-which-model-l/index.md) and
[The collet with no drawing](../log/2026-10-13-the-collet-with-no-drawing/index.md).

## The plates

- **The speed plate**, on the front of the stand, is headed MODEL-L and gives
  six spindle speeds in two rows: 390, 685 and 1140, then 785, 1370 and 2290.
  Each in the second row is twice the one above, so the drive is a three-step
  pulley and a motor with two speeds, the arrangement the catalogue gives for
  its high-speed machines, though with slightly different figures.
- **The serial number** is stamped into the top of the bed at the tailstock
  end, on the land beside the slot, with a dab of red paint after it. The
  maker's manual says to look for it on the right-hand end of the bed.
- **A cast plate on the headstock** reads Smart & Brown (Machine Tools) Ltd,
  Biggleswade, and under that: use oil only, recommended grade Mobil DTE Oil
  Light. The oil sold under that name today is an
  [ISO VG 32 circulating oil](https://www.mcmaster.com/oil/trade-name~mobil-dte-light).
- **A red badge** below it reads Smart & Brown, London.
- **A dealer's plate** on the stand reads Buck & Hickman Ltd, Engineers, with
  branches at Watford, London, Birmingham, Manchester, Bristol, Glasgow and
  Leeds.

The bed has a flat top with a slot down the middle, and the headstock and
tailstock clamp to it by round buttons in the slot. The castings are in the
rounded style of the 1950s. Both from Andrew.

## How old it is

1961. Bracehand Ltd, which carries on the Smart & Brown name, gave the year
for serial number S10312 when I wrote to them in October 2026, and confirmed
that it was built with the PDH headstock.

Before that I had estimated about 1960 from the numbers on record. The
[Sabel page](https://www.lathes.co.uk/sabel) at lathes.co.uk says Smart &
Brown numbered their machines in one sequence as they left the works,
whatever the model, and lists a few survivors: 8385 from 1955 and 9562 from
1958 among them. That is a little under four hundred machines a year, and at
that rate 10312 left about two years after 9562. The estimate was a year
short, which is about as close as that method could be expected to come.

## The two headstocks

**The lathe's own is of the PDH family.** The spindle nose has only a fine
thread, carrying a collet-closing cap that screws down inside the front
bearing's adjusting nut until nothing stands proud. There is no way to mount
a backplate on it. That is the catalogue's
[Type PDH](https://www.lathes.co.uk/smart-and-brown-model-L): bronze at the
front, a pair of preloaded ball races at the back that need no adjusting, a
bore of 29/32 in, and a nose that takes dead-length collets and nothing else.
This one's smallest bore, measured from the back, is the collet's seat at
23.84 mm, larger than the catalogue's figure.

The maker's parts book draws the bearings in one section for the PLH, PRH and
PDH, giving the PDH its own spindle (L4/4631). It shows a plain bush at the
front with two threaded parts in front of it at the nose, L4 905 and
L4 1936; a pair of angular-contact ball races at the back, marked "N.1906 135
A.C. E.P. type"; and the stepped pulley on two ball races of its own, marked
XLS 2" and XLS 2¼". It does not say which of the two parts at the nose sets
the front bearing. What the book is, and what it leaves out, is in
[A manual with no words](../log/2026-10-28-a-manual-with-no-words/index.md).

It also has a lever closer at the back end of the spindle, with a fluted
handwheel outboard of it, fixed to the spindle for turning it by hand. The
catalogue's text says the PDH has no draw-bar,
but its own
[photograph of the PDH](https://www.lathes.co.uk/smart-and-brown-model-L/page2.html)
shows the same arrangement as this lathe: a lever on a yoke at the back of
the spindle, a ring outboard of it, and a plain capped nose. A dead-length
collet is closed by pushing, not pulling: the cap holds the collet endwise
and a sleeve is pushed forward to close it. The parts book shows how: a
tube, L4 4632, runs the length of the spindle bore from the closer to the
collet. The cap is L4 763, turned by a pin spanner, L4 2808. The lever's
yoke rides on a ring of balls round the sleeve, with a small fitting on top,
XF 5B. A detail of the closer is marked as used up to July 1955 production,
and an older toggle closer is drawn and marked obsolete. The catalogue lists
a lever closer made for dead-length collets, the Type DL, which I take this
to be.
The LPH, the other headstock with these bearings, has its handle on the
front and works a brake and clutch with it, which this one has not.

That photograph also shows a small upright fitting on top of the back
bearing housing, where this lathe has an oil nipple.

Between the closer and the headstock are a ring nut with tommy-bar holes and
a disc with holes drilled in it. The knurled plunger on the front of the
headstock is what the parts book calls the pulley stop pin, L4 952: the
spindle lock. The pulley is stepped, for a flat belt.

**The spare is a PRH.** Painted green, so from another machine. Andrew has
compared it with the catalogue's cutaway of the PRH and finds everything he
can see matches: a hardened spindle in two adjustable bronze bearings, the
pulley on ball races of its own, a lever collet closer of the draw-in kind,
and a nose threaded to take a chuck. Its closer lever is missing, with only
the stub it fits to.

The parts book disagrees with the catalogue about the PRH's back bearing.
It draws the pair of ball races as common to the PLH, PRH and PDH, where the
catalogue gives the PLH and PRH two adjustable bronze bearings. Only the spare
itself can say which it has.

A lathe whose own headstock can hold only collets, and which has no collets,
can hold nothing. Andrew's reading is that an earlier owner bought the spare
headstock and the chuck for exactly that reason. The chuck's backplate fits
the spare's nose and not this one's.

## The drive

The stand has two rotary switches. Their plates read Reversing Switch, with
reverse, off and forward, and Change Pole Switch, with low, off and high: a
switch for a motor with two speeds, as the speed plate implies.

The motor that came with the lathe has one. Its plate reads Brook Motors
Limited, Huddersfield: frame C182, serial number Y17894, 400/440 volts, three
phase, 3.1 amps at full load, 1,430 r.p.m., 50 cycles, 2 horsepower. At
1,430 r.p.m. it would give the second row of the speed plate and nothing
would give the first. So either the lathe's own motor was replaced, or this
one never belonged to it.

The parts book draws four drives for the Model L: direct, by countershaft,
from a floor-mounted motor, and through a gearbox with a clutch. This lathe's
is the first, the motor on a hinged platform in the stand that a lever on the
front raises and lowers to slacken the belt. The book does not give the
belt's length.

The drive is a plain pair of three-step cones, one on the spindle and one on
the motor, with no countershaft. Andrew measured them:

| Pulley | Steps, diameter | Width of each step | Bore |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| On the spindle | 2.8, 3.8 and 4.8 in | 1.16 in | |
| Two loose ones for the motor, alike | 2.6, 3.6 and 4.6 in | 1.16 in | 1 in, keyed, as is the motor's shaft |

Each pair of steps adds up to 7.4 in, so one belt serves all three. From the
diameters alone, a motor at 1,430 r.p.m. gives about 775, 1,355 and 2,350
r.p.m., within three per cent of the plate's lower row, and one at half that
speed gives the upper row. So the original motor ran at about 1,430 and 715
r.p.m., and an inverter at 50 and 25 cycles would give both rows from the
Brook.

## The stand, the slide rest and the tailstocks

The stand is a cast cabinet with a tray round the top, a large door at the
front and a smaller one low on the left. It is not the pattern in the early
catalogue pictures, which has double doors and louvres. The slide rest is a
compound one worked by screw handles, with a swivelling top slide and a
pillar toolpost: the catalogue's Type CSP. The parts book draws the CSP in
two versions, with parallel gibs and with a taper gib; which this one has is
not yet recorded.

The tailstock on the bed is green, like the spare headstock, and came from
the same other machine. The second tailstock, loose in the tray, is the one
with set-over; what paint is left on it is a brighter green over red. So
neither tailstock is in the lathe's own colour.

## What a Model L looks like

From the catalogue pictures on the
[Model L page](https://www.lathes.co.uk/smart-and-brown-model-L), described
rather than copied. The bed is short and is carried on two pedestals above a
tray, on a cabinet with a pair of doors in front and louvres below them. The
flat belt comes up inside the pedestal under the headstock from a motor in the
cabinet, and the headstock casting curves over the pulley to guard it. There
is no carriage. The slide rest is a unit of its own, a cross-slide carrying a
swivelling top slide, clamped to the bed wherever the work needs it by a nut
underneath, and the tailstock clamps to the bed in the same way. The maker's
plate is on the front of the headstock pedestal. Machines of the 1970s are
the same lathe in squared-off castings on a fabricated stand.

## The Model L headstocks in the catalogue

The original six are all given as 4 in centre height. From the
[Model L pages](https://www.lathes.co.uk/smart-and-brown-model-L) at
lathes.co.uk.

| Type | How to know it | Spindle bearings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PLH | Handwheel draw-in collet closer. Nose 1¾ in × 8 t.p.i., bore 63/64 in. | Lapped, adjustable phosphor bronze. The pulley runs on ball bearings of its own. |
| PRH | A PLH with a lever collet closer in place of the handwheel. | As the PLH. |
| PSH | For small work: collets to 5/16 in only. | Not given. |
| PDH | No thread for a chuck: the nose has only a fine thread for a collet-closing cap, and it takes dead-length collets. | Bronze at the front, a pair of preloaded ball races at the back, needing no adjustment. |
| PGH, PFH | A short lever in front of the left-hand bearing, which engages a 3 to 1 reduction gear inside the pulley. The PFH is the same with a lever closer. | As the PDH. |
| LPH | One handle on the front that works brake, clutch and collet closer together. | Plain at the front, ball race at the back. |
| PRH/1 and late types | Take 5C collets. Nose 2 in × 8 t.p.i. on the PRH/1. | Angular-contact ball races at the nose, roller at the back. |

And the tailstocks: the TSC, a one-piece casting with a 1 in barrel and a
No. 1 Morse taper, which the parts book confirms, with a lead screw, as it calls the
barrel's screw, of ⅛ in pitch, changed to 1/10 in from a point it marks only
as "MT. 24"; the TUC, heavier, with a 1¼ in barrel, No. 2 Morse taper
and the sole plate for set-over; and the TTC, which carries small collets, for
production work and sensitive drilling.

## Telling it from the other models

"A Smart & Brown lathe" does not say much. The firm, at Biggleswade in
Bedfordshire, built lathes from the early 1940s into the 1990s, and they have
little in common beyond the name and the class of work they were meant for.
Which one this is decides how its spindle runs, how it is oiled, what fits its
nose and which manual applies.

Everything in the table is from Tony Griffiths' pages at
[lathes.co.uk](https://www.lathes.co.uk/smartbrown), put in my own words.
Sizes are centre height by distance between centres.

| Model | Made | Size | What to look for | Spindle bearings |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Model L](https://www.lathes.co.uk/smart-and-brown-model-L) | Early 1940s to the 1990s | About 4 in × 13½ in | A plain-turning lathe after a pre-war Boley: no leadscrew and no screwcutting. Usually a collet closer, often a lever-feed slide, a cut-off slide or a capstan in place of a tailstock. Many variants. | Depends on the headstock: lapped bronze on some, ball and roller races on others. |
| [Model M](https://www.lathes.co.uk/smart-and-brown-model-M) | 1946 to about 1959 | 4 in × 18 in | Also after a Boley. Changewheels on a swinging bracket, with the leadscrew running down the middle of the bed underneath, not along the front. Back gear, and a three-step flat-belt pulley. ### The quickest tests

- **The spindle nose.** A plain flange with a ring of cam sockets is a 1024.
  A thread means it is not.
- **The leadscrew.** None at all suggests a Model L. Under the middle of the
  bed points to a Model M, the only one described that way.
- **The stand.** A cast-iron cabinet with a speed lever on its front suggests
  a Model A, though some Model Ms had a gearbox in the base as well. Sheet
  metal with V-belts inside is a Sabel.
- **The centre height.** 4 in, 4½ in, 4¾ in or 5½ in narrows it at once.
