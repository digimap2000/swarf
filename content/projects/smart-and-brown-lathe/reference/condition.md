## Still to sort out

### Oilways, both headstocks

Total-loss. Each bearing has a pocket above it and nothing below: the
underside of the headstock is an open frame, and oil that has passed a
bearing drops out inside the casting. On the spare, oil put in at the top
runs straight through. On the fitted one, the inside of the casting is coated
with black oily dirt, heaviest under the front bearing, and filled pockets do
not fall that Andrew can see while the spindle is turned by hand. Left full,
both were still full a day later.

At the front, cleaned out, the pocket is a plain bore through the casting
with a clear gap at the bottom above the outside of the bronze bush, which
does not turn. Oil put in runs down into whatever space surrounds the bush,
so the pocket is a filler and not the reservoir. How oil then gets from that
space to the journal, through a hole or a wick in the bush, is not yet
known; a forum owner describes a sump and wick under Model L bronze
bearings. A full pocket over a full space would not fall in a day whether
the bearing was taking oil or not, so the overnight test said nothing about
the front. On 14 October Andrew wiped the spindle where it leaves the pulley
end of the front bearing and turned it a hundred times by hand: no fresh
oil, after that space had stood full for two days. The feed to the journal
is not proven and may be blocked. Three tests by hand have not settled it.
The maker's manual, which arrived as a parts book of drawings with no text,
does not settle it either: its section through the headstock shows the space
round the bush, and at its scale no hole or wick through the bush can be made
out. So it is to be answered from the lathe: the pocket and the space round
the bush emptied and the top of the bush looked at. See [A manual with no words](../log/2026-10-28-a-manual-with-no-words/index.md). At that end the bronze bush can be seen to be split, with
a slotted steel ring against it. Over the ball races at the back it points to a blocked way in,
most likely old grease. *Known from Andrew, his photograph of the underside,
and what he saw down both holes.*

### Grease at the back bearing

The pocket under the nipple on the lathe's own headstock was packed with
grease, against the maker's plate, which says oil only. Andrew cleaned it
out. The maker's manual draws the fitting there as an oil nipple, so the
bearing is meant to have oil, like the rest. It is a pair of ball races,
which grease does not harm the way it harms bronze, and its lift and end
float have been measured and it is sound: see below. *Andrew, his photograph
down the hole, and the manual.*

### Oil-hole plugs

A plain hole above each bronze bearing: one at the front of the lathe's own
headstock and two on the spare. The catalogue's cutaway of the PRH shows
each closed by a small knurled plug with a short stem, and the maker's manual
draws the same plug over the front bearing of this family, part L4 934,
without its sizes. All three are gone.
Keep the holes covered, and make three plugs once the holes are measured.
*Andrew, who looked at all the holes, and the cutaway he found.*

### Nipple at the back bearing

The hole over the back bearing of the lathe's own headstock is threaded, and
carries a nipple with a ball in its head, which Andrew has taken out. The
other three oil holes are plain. That bearing is a pair of ball races, and
the catalogue's photograph of the PDH shows a fitting in the same place. So
the nipple is the maker's: it goes back, and oil is put in through it. Its
thread is 6.3 mm across by Andrew's measure and about 26 to the inch by his
count: 1/4 in by 26. The maker's manual labels it a ¼ in BSF oil nipple,
which settles it against the cycle thread of the same size and pitch. The hole is
threaded for about 10 mm and runs the full depth of the casting, ending on a
silver surface that does not turn with the spindle: the outside of the
bearing, either the outer ring of a race or a spacer between the two. No way
for oil to pass is visible from above. That would explain a pocket that
held thin oil for a day, with grease round the races to stop it. The manual says nothing of how much oil it takes or how often. *Andrew, with the part, and the catalogue's
photograph.*

### Bearing adjusting rings

The notched ring behind each nose is bruised beside its notches, as if driven
round with a punch. The cutaway shows these rings set the bronze bearings.
Leave them alone, learn from the spindle play what the punching did, and make
a C-spanner to fit before either is moved.

On the lathe's own headstock the castellated ring at the nose was loose when
the lathe arrived. It turns by hand, runs on along its thread and stops. The
front bearing showed no lift on a 0.01 mm indicator with the ring in that
state, so something else is holding the bush for now; which ring sets the
clearance and which locks it the manual does not say: its section shows two
threaded parts in front of the bush, L4 905 and L4 1936, without a word on
either. Until it is answered the ring stays as found and the spindle does
not run under power.
*Photographs of both noses, the cutaway, and Andrew.*

### Spindle oil

The maker's plate asks for Mobil DTE Oil Light, an ISO VG 32. To hand are
Shell Morlina S2 BL 10 (VG 10) and Shell Tellus 68 (VG 68). The thin one is
for turning by hand and measuring, the Tellus for the slideways. For the
spindle under power, Westway Light Machine Oil ISO 32: a mineral oil for
enclosed and circulating systems, which its label lists for use where Mobil
DTE Light is specified and as safe with bronze bushings and yellow metals.
That equivalence is Westway's statement and not the lathe maker's. Mixing the
two Shell oils would have come to about the right viscosity, but one has a
zinc additive and the other is made without, and I found nothing to say they
sit well together. *Andrew, the oils' data sheets, and the Westway label.*

### Bed

It came with the headstock, slide rest and tailstock resting on it, not
fixed, and itself resting loose on the stand for transport. Its bolts came
with it; there were no shims under its feet. Andrew lifted them off and cleaned it with T-Cut and a Brillo pad. A
groove is cut across its front bevel near the headstock end, which looks
deliberate to both of us. About 32¾ in long, by Andrew's tape as I read it
from his photograph. To measure for wear, and oil against rust. *Andrew, and
his photographs.*

### Collets

None came with it, and they are what Andrew most wants the lathe for. The
lathe's own headstock takes the Crawford 5159, a dead-length collet, and a
plastic print made to Crawford's figures locks a bar in it. I have found none
for sale. The spare takes draw-in collets of a published pattern. Details and
what is still to measure are on the [collets page](collets/index.md).
*Andrew's measurements and trial prints, and published figures.*

### Belt

There is none. Bracehand give the belt as 25 mm wide, which suits pulley
steps 1.16 in wide and agrees with the catalogue's one inch, and 180 mm long
for the direct-drive lathe or 1,553 mm for the 4:1 drive. This lathe is
direct drive by its pulleys: a three-step cone on the motor driving a
three-step cone on the spindle. 180 mm is too short to reach from the motor
platform, and the manual gives no length, so it is to come from measuring
with the motor on its platform, and the belt from a belt supplier. *Andrew,
Bracehand, and the manual.*

### Motor pulleys

Two came with the lathe, alike: steps of 2.6, 3.6 and 4.6 in, each 1.16 in
wide, bored 1 in with a keyway to fit the motor's shaft. Both look like plain cast
iron, unpainted, rusted all over, and on each a piece is broken out of the
edge at the end of the smallest step. They are in citric acid, bores greased,
to take the rust off. Then each chip is measured from the edge: a 1 in belt
on a 1.16 in step leaves about 0.08 in clear at each side, so a chip inside
that only needs its edge dressed, and one beyond it is in the belt's path.
The better pulley goes on the motor. *Andrew, and his photographs.*

### Motor

Out of the stand. A single-speed Brook, 2 h.p., 400/440 V three-phase, where
the lathe's change-pole switch and speed plate are for a motor with two
speeds. It was loose when Andrew got the lathe.

The terminal box, opened on 29 October: a black moulded terminal board with
three studs, one lead from the windings on each and no links between them,
and an earth screw beside it. So the windings are connected inside the motor
and it is wound for the one voltage on its plate. No supply cable is
fitted. Through the hole in the middle of the board, round the leads, an
amber-coloured sheet can be seen. It is not identified: likely varnished
cloth or pressboard, but it is left alone until it is, under the rule for
fibrous linings in old electrical gear.

Resistance between the three studs, by Andrew's multimeter on 29 October:
10.5 ohms across one pair and open circuit across the other two. That is the
pattern of a star-wound motor with one winding, or its lead, broken: the two
readings that pass through it are open, and the one that does not reads two
good windings in series, about 5 ohms each. A delta winding with one break
would still read across every pair. So the motor cannot run as it is. Where
the break is, at the stud, in the lead or at the star point inside, is not
yet known. There is no insulation tester here, so insulation to the frame is
not yet measured. There is a platform for it in
the stand with a lever to slacken the belt, and two stepped pulleys for it.
Andrew's inverter gives up to 415 V three-phase from a single-phase supply
and is rated at 2.2 kW, which is more than the Brook's 2 h.p., about 1.5 kW. Whether the
two switches can be kept as signals to the inverter is settled at the
electrical stage. *Its plate, and the plates on the switches.*

### Coolant

A pipe, and a tray made as a basin with a brass drain plug. Pump and tank not
yet looked for. Andrew wants it working in the end. *Andrew.*

### Chuck backplate

A three-jaw chuck came with the lathe, and a backplate for it threaded for
the nose of the spare headstock, the only one of the two that can carry it,
and otherwise unmachined. Machine it on the spare's own spindle
once the lathe runs on that headstock, so that it runs true to it. *Andrew.*

### Spare headstock, a PRH

Complete but for its closer lever. Oil put in its pockets runs straight
through. Its bearings have not been measured. The catalogue gives a PRH two
bronze bearings; the maker's manual draws its back bearing as a pair of ball
races, in a section it marks as common to the PLH, PRH and PDH. Which this
one has is to be found from the spare itself. It is the only headstock here
that can hold work, so it is not parked. *Photograph, and Andrew.*

### Closer yoke

The maker's manual shows the closer's yoke riding on a ring of balls round
the sleeve, with a small fitting on top of it, part XF 5B, which looks like
an oil point. It has not been on any list of oil points here. Find whether
the fitting is on this lathe and what it is, when the closer is next looked
at. *The manual.*

## Sound so far

### Spindle bearings

On the lathe's own headstock, clamped to the bed, with a 0.01 mm dial
indicator:

| Reading | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Lift at the front bearing, on the collet-closing cap | None the indicator can show |
| Lift at the back bearing, on the spindle between the bearing and the closer | None the indicator can show |
| End float | 0.01 mm, one division |

So the preload on the back pair of ball races has survived, whatever the
grease did, and the front bronze bearing is close. Whether it gets oil is
another matter: see Oilways. *Andrew, 12 and 14 October 2026.*

The one with set-over. Loose in the tray, most of its paint gone, held

against rust and leave it. *Photograph, and Andrew.*
