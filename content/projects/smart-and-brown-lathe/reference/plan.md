## The aim

Andrew is the customer, and he wants both: the engineering done correctly,
and a beautiful finish, with the painting and plating done in his own
workshop.

What the lathe is to be is his to say; how to get it there is mine. Where
finish and fit pull against each other, fit wins, and the finish is got some
other way. A slideway is never painted to the edge by sanding the edge; a
bearing is never disturbed because the casting round it would be easier to
paint without it.

## The rules I am working to

1. **Look before touching.** The state it arrived in is evidence, and cleaning
   destroys it: where oil has been reaching and where it has not, where swarf
   has packed, where something has leaked. The original finish is evidence
   too. Its colour, what was painted, what was bright, what was plated or
   blued, where each plate sat: all recorded before any of it is removed.
2. **Run it and measure it before it comes apart.** The lathe will be stripped
   for finishing whatever is found. So it should come apart once, with the
   list of what to put right already written, and with a set of measurements
   to prove the finished machine against. A lathe turning under power reports
   on its own bearings and alignments in an afternoon; in boxes it reports
   nothing.
3. **No fit is altered without evidence.** Everything comes off to be
   finished. But a bearing, a slideway or a taper that measures well is
   cleaned and put back exactly as it was. Most of what is ruined in a
   restoration was ruined by improving something that was fine.
4. **One assembly on the bench at a time.** Its parts are bagged and labelled
   together, go to paint and plating as a set, and are back together and
   proved before the next is opened.
5. **Finish never touches a working surface.** No paint, filler, plating or
   blasting grit on a slideway, a bearing, a taper, a thread, a face that one
   casting locates on another by, a graduation or an oilway. Each is masked
   or plugged before the part goes to the booth or the tank, and checked when
   it comes back.
6. **Nothing that cannot be undone on a guess.** Scraping, grinding,
   machining and drilling need a measurement that calls for them, and
   Andrew's agreement, written down first. So do blasting and plating of any
   part that has a fit on it.
7. **Maker's figures or none.** Clearances, oil grades and the order a thing
   comes apart come from the maker's manual for this model. Where I do not
   have it, I say so rather than supply a figure of my own.
8. **Say what I expect before I look.** Each job is set with a prediction, and
   the log records whether it was right.

## The finish, and where it can hurt

This is general workshop practice, not anything Smart & Brown wrote down, and
each point will be settled part by part when the time comes.

- **Grit.** Blasting media bed themselves into cast iron and bronze and stay
  there as lapping compound. A slideway is never blasted, nor a casting with
  a bearing still in it, and every oil hole is plugged first.
- **Thickness.** Plating adds metal. Nothing with a fit is plated: not a
  journal, a bore, a taper, a feed screw or a gib.
- **Hardness.** Pickling and electroplating can leave hydrogen in hardened
  steel and make it brittle. Hardened parts and springs are either baked
  straight after plating or left unplated.
- **Filler and paint** stop short of every joint face, so that castings still
  meet metal to metal.
- **The spindle.** Whether a headstock is painted with its spindle in place
  and masked, or stripped bare, depends on what the bearings measure and on
  what the manual says about getting the spindle out. One owner of a Model L
  got his out three-quarters of an inch and no further.

## What there is to work with

- **Supply.** Single-phase, with inverters to make three-phase from it, one
  of them on a socket kept for jobs like this.
- **Measuring.** Dial indicators reading to 0.01 mm, micrometers, bore gauges
  and a precision level. Enough for the tests in stage 5. Readings go in the
  record in millimetres, as taken, whatever the lathe was built in.
- **Making.** A Colchester Chipmaster and an old South Bend lathe, a Centec 2A
  universal mill, a hydraulic press, TIG welding, and an engine crane for
  lifting. A worn or missing part can be made here.
- **Finishing.** A paint booth, and plating, both in house.
- **Drive.** One of those inverters gives up to 415 V three-phase, which
  suits the 400/440 V motor that came with the lathe as it is.
- **Money.** The lathe cost £150 and the budget is small on purpose: the point
  is the engineering, so a part is mended or made before one is bought. The
  one thing I may yet argue for buying is the maker's manual, if no free copy
  turns up.

## The order of work

| Stage | What happens | It ends when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0. Identify | Which Model L it is, what came with it, what is known of its past, and Andrew's reading of its condition. Nothing moved. | The headstock, drive, slide rest and tailstocks are named and the maker's manual is in hand. |
| 1. Look it over by hand | With no power: the slideways cleaned and oiled only so that things can be moved without harm, then everything turned and traversed by hand. Wear that can be measured without power is measured. Oil, belt and wiring inspected. The finish recorded as found. | Every assembly has a line in the condition register. |
| 2. Lubricate | Old oil out, the maker's grades in, and every oil point shown to deliver. | Oil is known to reach each bearing and slide. |
| 3. Electrical | The motor plate decides how it will be supplied. Wiring and switchgear inspected; earth continuity and insulation resistance measured before anything is energised. | It passes, and a supply for it is in place. |
| 4. First run | No load, lowest speed, short runs, working up. Noise, vibration and bearing temperature watched. | It runs at every speed without distress, or the reason it cannot is known. |
| 5. Measure | The standard alignment tests for a plain lathe: spindle runout and end float, headstock to bed, the slide rest, tailstock to headstock. | There is a table of its errors, which is both the list of what to put right and the mark the finished machine has to meet. |
| 6. Strip and put right | Assembly by assembly: dismantled, photographed, cleaned, inspected. Whatever stages 1, 4 and 5 condemned is repaired or remade. Nothing else with a fit on it is changed. | Each assembly's faults are closed and its parts are ready for finishing. |
| 7. Finish | Castings stripped, filled and painted. Bright parts polished or plated. Working surfaces masked throughout. | Every part is finished and its working surfaces are as they went in. |
| 8. Reassemble and measure again | Built back up with the maker's clearances and oils, and the tests of stage 5 repeated. | It measures as well as it did before it came apart, or better. |
| 9. Prove | Test cuts, and a part made to a drawing. | It does the work. |

The stages are not a staircase. If the lathe cannot be run safely as it
stands, stages 4 and 5 are done as far as they can be by hand and it goes to
the strip with a shorter list.

## Where it stands

**Stage 1: looking it over by hand, with no power.** The lathe is
[named](identification.md), and what is wrong with it so far is in the
[condition register](condition.md). The headstock is back on the bed and
clamped. The slide rest and tailstock are off. There is no power to it.

A few decisions stand alongside the stages:

- **Headstock.** The lathe's own headstock, the PDH, is to be proved first,
  because Andrew wants a good collet system more than a chuck. Its collet is
  known, a Crawford 5159, and is on the [collets page](collets/index.md); a
  steel one waits until the spindle has run. The spare PRH is not parked: it
  is the only headstock here that can hold work in steel.
- **Spares.** The set-over tailstock is parked, to be oiled against rust
  and left.
- **The manual.** Still not in hand, and needed before any headstock is taken
  apart. I recommended a reprinted data pack at £67. Andrew thought that too
  much for paper that often turns up for nothing and sent me back to look,
  and he was right to: Bracehand Ltd, which carries on the Smart & Brown
  name, lists an
  [operator's and parts manual](https://www.smartandbrownlathes.com/our-lathes/system-l-lathe/)
  for the Model L at £13.50. I have found no free copy. It should say how
  each bearing is meant to be oiled and how the closer is set, which
  experiment has not.
- **Spindle oil.** The maker's own, Mobil DTE Light, can be had decanted by
  the [litre](https://www.ebay.co.uk/itm/322454043460) at about £16. Andrew
  found Westway's Light Machine Oil ISO 32 at about £25 for a five-litre
  can, and I chose that, and it is on order: a mineral oil for circulating
  systems, which
  its maker lists for use where Mobil DTE Light is specified and as safe
  with bronze. The spindle does not turn under power on anything thinner or
  thicker.
- **Drive.** My intention is that the two original switches go on doing
  their jobs as signals to the inverter, not as switches in the motor's
  supply: forward, off and reverse from one, and two set frequencies from the
  other, which would give back both rows of the speed plate from a motor
  with one speed. To be settled at stage 3.
- **Coolant.** Andrew wants it working in the end. What survives of it is
  looked at when the stand is surveyed.

## Done on 14 October

The session at the headstock settled most of what it set out to. The
results are on the [condition register](condition.md), the
[collets page](collets/index.md) and the
[identification page](identification.md). In short:

- **Spindle bearings sound.** No lift at either end on a 0.01 mm indicator,
  and 0.01 mm end float.
- **The nipple over the back bearing is the maker's,** so three plugs are
  missing, not four.
- **The front bearing's oil feed is not proven.** The pocket fills a space
  round the bronze bush; no fresh oil came out at the bush's pulley end
  after a hundred turns. No more tests by hand: the manual's drawing comes
  first. Its castellated ring was loose on arrival and stays as found.
- **The cap alone sets the collet closer.** The fluted handwheel only turns
  the spindle by hand. The collet's seat begins about 41.3 mm in.
- **The drive is a direct pair of three-step cones,** and the speeds they
  give agree with the plate.

## The next workshop session: the stand

One visit, at the stand, with no power to anything. Each group says what it
settles. Nothing here touches the headstock.

### The motor: can it run from the inverter

1. **The terminal box.** Lid off: a photograph of the terminals and any
   links between them. If there is fibrous board or woven tape inside, leave
   it alone and tell me.
2. **Insulation,** each phase to the frame, at 500 V on your tester.
3. **Winding resistance,** each pair of phases.
4. **The shaft by hand.** Does it turn freely and quietly, and is there any
   play?

*Settles* whether the Brook can be put on the inverter as it is, and how its
windings are to be connected.

**I expect** three terminals in star for 400/440 V, insulation well above
a megohm, three resistances within a few per cent of each other, and a free
shaft.

### The switches: can they become the inverter's controls

5. **Both rotary switches,** covers off: a photograph of the contacts and
   wiring of each, and the state of the wire's insulation.

*Settles* whether they can be kept, as signals to the inverter, or are to be
replaced behind their original plates.

### The bed: is it true enough to measure from

6. **Twist, loose and bolted.** The bed came loose on the stand, unbolted for
   transport. With the precision level across the bed at the headstock end,
   the middle and the tailstock end: once as it rests, and again after it is
   bolted down evenly on clean faces. Any shims or packing that came under
   its feet go back where they were.
7. **Wear.** Where the undersides of the slot's lips are machined, the
   thickness of each lip by micrometer every two inches, with the part under
   the headstock as the reference, since nothing has slid there.

*Settles* whether the bed needs anything before the alignment tests, and
whether bolting it to the stand twists it, which would call for shims under
its feet.

**I expect** no twist the level can show on so short a bed, loose or
bolted, and the lips
within a thousandth or two of an inch along their length.

## After that

The slide rest and the two tailstocks make one later session: each slide and
barrel tried and measured. The spare headstock waits behind the lathe's own.

Meanwhile, without the workshop: Bracehand answered the morning after I
wrote. They gave the lathe's year as 1961, offered the manual at £17.50 and a
used 5159 collet with a 1.5 mm bore at £12.60, and gave the belt's size. The
manual is being bought. Andrew turned down the collet, since used 5159s with
more useful bores are on eBay for not much more, and the belt is to come from
a belt supplier once its length is known. A real 5159 is still what I most
want: it will give the back bearing, the waist and the slits that Crawford's
table leaves out, and the length of its nose will test the rule that placed
the taper on the plastic one.
