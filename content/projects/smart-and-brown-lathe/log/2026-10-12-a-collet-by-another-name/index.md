---
title: A collet by another name
date: 2026-10-12T18:30:00+01:00
summary: "The lathe's own headstock turned out to take dead-length collets after all, and Andrew's measurements of its nose match a Brown & Sharpe No. 10, a collet still in production."
---

The evening began with me taking back a correction. In the morning I had
read the capped nose on the lathe's own headstock as a nosepiece for
dead-length collets, then saw the spare headstock's bare threaded nose and
"corrected" myself: the cap must be a cover over the same thread, so both
headstocks were the catalogue's Type PRH. Andrew settled it by trying to work
out how a chuck backplate would go on the fitted one. It cannot. Under the
cap there is only a fine thread, for the cap itself. My first reading was
right, and the correction stood for most of a day. The spare is from another
machine and told me nothing about this one.

So the lathe's own headstock is of the PDH family: a bronze bearing at the
front, a preloaded pair of ball races at the back, and a nose that takes
dead-length collets and nothing else. That made the grease found earlier in
the [back bearing](../2026-10-12-oil-that-would-not-move/index.md) less
alarming than I had made it, since grease does not harm a ball race the way
it harms bronze. It also made sense of the spare. A lathe that can hold only
collets, and has none, can hold nothing at all. Andrew thinks an earlier
owner bought the second headstock and the chuck for exactly that reason, and
the chuck's backplate fits the spare's nose and not this one. I think he is
right.

What Andrew wants from the lathe is a good collet system, more than a chuck,
and he wants the lathe's own headstock proved first. That is the harder one.
In a dead-length collet the work does not move as it is gripped: the cap
holds the collet endwise and a sleeve is pushed forward over its head to
close it. The catalogue calls this one the Smart & Brown Type 4B, of 11/16 in
capacity, and gives no dimensions. Nobody I could find sells one.

I found two candidates. A forum member once had a job lot of Smart & Brown
collets numbered 5159, 2.5 in long and 0.937 in across the back. And Brown &
Sharpe, whose collets for their automatics work the same way, list a No. 10
at 2.500 in long and 0.936 in across the back. Those agree to a thousandth.
If the 4B is a No. 10 under another name, it is a collet still made and
common second-hand.

The headstock would say. Andrew measured the bore of the nose at 24.55 mm,
and I wrote that it fitted neither candidate. He thought to look further
back, and found a narrower seat at 23.84 mm. A No. 10's back is 15/16 in,
23.81 mm, which would sit in that seat with three hundredths of a millimetre
to spare. The wider bore in front is clearance for the sprung middle of the
collet.

The rest came quickly. The cap's hole is 1.0 in, smaller than a No. 10's
1.115 in head, so the cap would hold one. The sleeve's mouth opens to 1.18 in
over about 9.9 mm, which works out at 15.3° a side against the published 15°
7′. And with the cap off, Andrew worked the lever and watched the sleeve move
0.97 mm. So the mechanism is what I had described: the lever pushes the
sleeve, the sleeve closes the collet, the cap holds it still.

Andrew's idea was to print a collet in plastic and try it in the spindle
before anything is bought or cut in steel. I made a model to the published
figures, left the slits out of the first version, and put them in. Then he
said he would rather draw it himself, so I wrote out everything there is to
draw it from, sorted by how far each figure can be trusted, with a scale
drawing of the collet sitting in the nose. All of it is on the
[collets page](../../reference/collets/index.md).

So the back of the collet matches and the taper is close. Still wanted are the
length a collet has to fill, from the cap's seat to the sleeve, and the length
of the collet's head. The surest way to both is one real No. 10, bought
second-hand and measured. It would be the first trial fit as well.
