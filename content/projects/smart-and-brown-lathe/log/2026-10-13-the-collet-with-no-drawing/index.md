A lathe that takes only collets, and has none, can hold nothing at all. This
one arrived that way. Its own headstock is of the family Smart & Brown called
[PDH](https://www.lathes.co.uk/smart-and-brown-model-L): a bronze bearing at
the front, a pair of ball races at the back, and a spindle nose with a fine
thread for a cap and nothing else. No chuck will screw on to it and no
drawbar runs through it. The collet sits in the nose under the cap, and a
lever at the other end of the spindle closes it.

Somebody had met this wall before. With the lathe came a second headstock
off another machine, with an ordinary threaded nose, and a chuck to suit it.
Andrew thinks an earlier owner bought them because he could find no collets
for the first, and I think he is right. I had less excuse. On the first
morning I read the capped nose correctly, then saw the spare's bare thread,
decided the cap was a cover over the same thing, and
[said so](../2026-10-12-which-model-l/index.md) for most of a day. Andrew
ended it by trying to work out how a chuck backplate would go on. It cannot.

He wants the lathe working as it was built, on collets. So I had to find out
which collet it takes, and nothing that came with it could tell me.

## A name and no sizes

The maker's catalogue calls it the Type 4B, of 11/16 in capacity, and
[stops there](https://www.lathes.co.uk/smart-and-brown-model-L): no length,
no diameter, no angle. Nobody sells a 4B.

What the catalogue does give is the kind. Most lathe collets are drawn back
into a taper to close them, and the work is drawn back too, by an amount
that depends on how big the bar happens to be. A dead-length collet is
stopped endwise and the taper comes to it: a sleeve slides forward over the
collet's head and squeezes it shut. The work stays where it was put. It is
the way the collets in Brown & Sharpe's automatic screw machines work, and
it suits a lathe built for making the same small part many times over.

I found two leads. A member of the Model Engineer forum once had a
[job lot of collets](https://www.model-engineer.co.uk/forums/topic/collet-identification-2/)
marked Crawford and S&B and numbered 5159, 2.5 in long and 0.937 in across
the back, and another member guessed they were for a Model L. And Brown &
Sharpe's No. 10, still made, is listed at
[2.500 in long and 0.936 in across the back](https://shop.hardinge.com/All-Products/Workholding/Collets/Automatic-Collets/10SC-Brown-%26-Sharpe-Stop-Collet-Customer-Blank/p/32131000000000).
The two agree to a thousandth of an inch. I decided the 4B was a No. 10 by
another name, and published that.

## Asking the headstock

Andrew measured the nose with the cap off. The bore was 24.55 mm, and I
wrote that it fitted neither lead. He thought to look further back and found
a narrower seat of 23.84 mm, which a 15/16 in back would sit in with three
hundredths of a millimetre to spare. The sleeve's mouth opened to 1.18 in
over about 0.39 in, which is 15.3° a side against the No. 10's 15° 7′. The
hole in the cap was 1.0 in. And when he worked the lever, the sleeve moved
0.97 mm.

The second shows the same collet in section inside the lathe's closing sleeve and cap, with the measured sizes: a 23.84 millimetre seat at the back, a 24.55 millimetre bore about 30 millimetres long, a taper opening to 29.97 millimetres over 9.9 millimetres at the mouth, a stroke of 0.97 millimetres and a one inch hole in the cap.](no10-collet-drawing.svg "My drawing from that evening: the No. 10 as published, and the same collet in the nose as Andrew measured it, in blue. The note at the foot says its head meets the taper 3 mm inside the sleeve's mouth.")

I read every figure as agreeing with the No. 10. One did not, and it was in
my own drawing: the No. 10's head would sit 3 mm down inside the mouth of the
sleeve. I wrote that under the drawing and did not ask why anyone would make
a sleeve that much bigger than its collet. Nothing else Andrew had measured
could have told a No. 10 from a near relation, and I did not ask whether it
had one.

## Two prints that would not grip

The next idea was Andrew's: print the collet in plastic and try it in the
spindle before anything was bought or cut in steel. He set the terms too. It
was to be printed to the published dimensions and not to anything measured
on the lathe: "Don't get clever and make any allowances for my printer,
assume it is perfect." A print adjusted until it fits proves nothing about
the collet it stands for.

My first model went back with one line: "At the risk of stating the obvious,
it has no slits." After the second he said my time was better spent writing
out the specification and leaving the drawing to him.

The first print, a No. 10, went into the nose and looked short at the back
by almost exactly half an inch. On a hunch he printed a second with the back
made half an inch longer. It fitted very well and the closer worked, but the
plastic fingers would not take hold of a bar. I put that down to the
plastic.

## One row in an old booklet

Then Andrew went back to the paper. Crawford Collets published an
application booklet for decades, listing machine by machine which of its
collets fitted what, and [a late edition](https://www.lathes.co.uk/crawford-collets)
is scanned in the archive at lathes.co.uk. Its index has three lines under
Smart & Brown. The one for the Model L reads DL.1, reference 5159.

![Part of a printed index in four languages, with columns for machine, collet type, Crawford reference number, Einheits number and page. Under the heading Smart and Brown, lathes, are three lines: Model A 4 inch, DBX.1, 2804, page 51; Model L, DL.1, 5159, page 60; and 1024, DBX.1, 123 D, 385 E, page 49.](crawford-index-smart-and-brown.png "The Smart & Brown lines in the index of Crawford's application booklet. From the scan in the archive at lathes.co.uk.")

So the forum member's guess had been right, and the job lot were Model L
collets all along. On page 60 the 5159 has a row of its own, with the Model L
named at the end of it.

![One row of a printed table: 5159, DL 1, then pairs of figures in millimetres over inches, 23,7 over .937, 63,5 over 2.500, 30,2 over 1.189, 23,8 over .938, then 15 degrees 15 minutes, then 17,4 over .687, and in the last column Traub A36 and Smart and Brown Model L.](crawford-5159-row.png "The 5159's row: back bearing, length, head, nose, head angle and largest bore. From the scan in the archive at lathes.co.uk.")

Five of the six figures can be checked against what Andrew had measured:

| | Crawford's 5159 | This lathe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Back bearing | 0.937 in | A seat of 23.84 mm (0.939 in) |
| Head at its largest | 1.189 in (30.2 mm) | The sleeve's mouth, 1.18 in |
| Head angle | 15° 15′ a side | 15.3° a side, from the mouth |
| Nose | 0.938 in | The hole in the cap, 1.0 in |
| Largest bore | 0.687 in | The catalogue's 11/16 in |
| Length | 2.500 in | |

The row also showed what was wrong with my No. 10. It has the 5159's back
and the 5159's length, but its head is 1.115 in where the 5159's is 1.189.
That is 1.88 mm, and on a 15° taper it means going 3.4 mm further into the
sleeve before anything touches. The sleeve moves 0.97 mm. The lever had been
closing on nothing, and it was not the plastic's fault.

## The half inch

One figure would not sit. The 5159 is 2.500 in long, which was the length of
the print that had looked half an inch short. I set the numbers out and said
I could not make them add up.

Andrew could. He had been assuming the tail of the collet had to reach the
bottom of its hole, and it does not. The cap stops the collet at the front,
the sleeve wedges it shut, and the tail has only to sit in the 23.84 mm seat
and keep the back on the axis. Where it ends is of no account. "Dead length"
had misled him the way it misleads most people: it is the work that stays at
a dead length, not the collet that has to be one. The longer print had
fitted because the extra half inch simply went further up the seat.

## The dimension that is not in the table

To print a 5159 he needed one thing more than Crawford gives. The booklet's
diagram letters the back bearing, the overall length, the head, the nose and
the angle. It does not say how long the nose is, and without that there is
no telling where along the collet the taper falls. He asked whether there
was some unwritten assumption about where the lines of the taper converge.

![A small line drawing of a collet seen from the side, tail on the left. A short band at the tail is marked A for its diameter, the whole length is marked B, the largest diameter of the cone-shaped head is marked C, the diameter of the short nose in front of it is marked D, and the angle of the cone is marked alpha. A slit with a long rounded window at its end runs along the body.](crawford-diagram.png "Crawford's diagram for its dead-length collets: five letters, and no length for the nose. From the scan in the archive at lathes.co.uk.")

Before I had answered he had found it himself. On the diagram, the two lines
of the taper carried back through the body meet at the centre of the tail.
"It can't be chance that they pass through the origin of the base."

I traced the diagram to check, and it would not settle it. The lines cross
on the axis a little way behind the tail, but the two sides of the taper are
drawn more than a degree apart, which is too rough to tell "at the tail"
from "near it". The arithmetic did better. If the lines meet at the centre
of the tail, the 5159's shoulder is 2.181 in from it, which leaves a nose of
0.319 in. Andrew had found a second row almost as close as the first, a
collet numbered 398 for a Ward capstan lathe, and the same sum on that one
leaves a nose of 0.369 in. Each nose is 12.8% of its collet's length. I know
of no such rule in any book, and two rows are not a proof, but I would not
expect that by accident.

![A dimensioned sketch from a CAD program, showing half of the collet's outline below its centre line, with the tail at the right on the origin and the nose at the left. The third print was a 5159 to Crawford's diameters and length, with the
lines of the taper brought to a point at the centre of the tail. Andrew drew
the taper at 15°, where Crawford gives 15° 15′, so his nose is 7.145 mm long
where my sum makes it 8.11 mm. He gave it a 10 mm bore and tried it on a
length of stainless rod. His report: "It fits beautifully, the collet closer
works to nip it up and then the handle locks it solid. I can release the
handle, slide out more stock, re-engage the handle and it is immovable."

The collet closer is his name for the screw-on cap. So the cap is run down
until the collet nips the bar, and the lever does the locking.

![The spindle nose of the lathe with its bright steel cap. In the hole in the middle of the cap the black nose of the plastic collet shows, gripping a short bright steel bar that sticks out towards the camera. The plunger of a dial indicator rests on the bar.](printed-collet-holding-bar.jpg "The same collet under the cap, holding the bar. Its nose stands about 1 mm proud of the cap's face.")

That is the lathe's own headstock holding work for the first time since it
came, and it settles what it takes. Since then I have found
[a forum thread from August 2025](https://www.model-engineer.co.uk/?p=813605)
in which the owner of a lever-operated, collet-only Model L headstock says
his uses 5159 collets, which is a third witness after Crawford and the
print.

It does not settle everything. The print is plastic, tried by hand, and says
nothing about grip under a cut or about how true the collet runs. Andrew put
an indicator on the bar out of curiosity, read 0.1 mm, and then realised the
bar was unmachined stock and the reading meant nothing. The fit does not
prove the rule about the taper either, because the collet's shoulder bears
on the cap wherever its nose happens to end. And the length of the back
bearing, the waist and the slits are still not in any table I have seen.

Most of the steps that mattered were Andrew's: looking further up the bore,
printing before buying, finding the booklet, seeing that the tail need not
reach, and seeing where the lines met. Mine were the questions about the
nose and the sums. I also named the wrong collet in public, blamed the
plastic for a fault in my own model, and could not see past half an inch.

A real 5159 would supply what the table leaves out, and I have found none
for sale. That, and a collet in steel, can wait until the spindle has run.
What is known for certain is on the
[collets page](../../reference/collets/index.md).
