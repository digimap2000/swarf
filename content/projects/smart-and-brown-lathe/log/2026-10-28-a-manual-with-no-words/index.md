For a fortnight one question has been waiting on a book. Oil poured into the
pocket over the front spindle bearing goes into the space round the bronze
bush, and then, as far as anyone can tell, nowhere: after a hundred turns by
hand none had come out at the end of the bush. Three tests had not settled
whether the feed to the journal was blocked or simply slow, and I stopped
Andrew trying a fourth. The maker's drawing would come first.

The manual came from Bracehand Ltd, who carry on the Smart & Brown name, for
£17.50 posted. Their listing calls it an operator's and parts manual, and I
wrote on the plan that it should say how each bearing is meant to be oiled
and how the closer is set. I expected an oiling chart, a page on the spindle
bearings and, with luck, a paragraph on which ring at the nose does what.

At the foot, two lines asking for the model and serial number in any correspondence and for the page and indicating number when ordering spare parts.](manual-title-page.png "The title page. Andrew scanned every page, and this is what it is. After the title page come
sixty-nine sheets of engineering drawings, sections and elevations, each with
a heading and a page number, and every part on them ringed with a number such
as L4 920. There is no list to say what the numbers are. There is no oiling
chart, no clearance, no torque, no order of dismantling, and not a sentence
addressed to the reader except on the title page: quote the model and serial
number, "to be found on the R.H end of bed", and when ordering spares, quote
the page number and the indicating number. It is a book for ordering parts
from Smart & Brown, made for someone with Smart & Brown at the other end of
the telephone.

The numbering begins at page three. Nothing in the book says what pages one
and two were.

So my expectation was wrong in kind, not in detail. The rest of this is what
a book of drawings with no words can still tell you, which turns out to be a
fair amount, and what it cannot.

## The whole range on paper

It covers the Model L as a family rather than any one machine. Four drives:
direct, by countershaft, from a floor-mounted motor, and through a gearbox
with a clutch in it. The headstocks: the PLH with its handwheel drawbar, the
PRH, the PDH, the later PRH/1 and the LPH with its single handle. Three
tailstocks, the TSC, TUC and TTC. Slide rests by the dozen, from the plain
compound CSP to capstan slides and a four-way toolbox slide. Then the suds
tank and pump, a one-inch bar feed, a fixed steady, a hand tool rest, and
grinding and milling attachments with their own drives.

This lathe is the first kind of drive, and the drawing of it shows what
Andrew found in the stand: the motor on a hinged platform, raised and lowered
by a lever on the front, so that the belt can be slackened without a spanner.
It does not give the belt's length.

The pages that matter most are four sheets for the lathe's own headstock: a
section drawn as common to the PLH, PRH and PDH, a side elevation, an end
view, and two sheets for the PDH's collet closer.

## What it settles

**The plunger is a spindle lock.** On the front of the headstock is a knurled
plunger that I had taken, from its position, to be a lock for the spindle.
The end view has a part-section through it, headed "pulley stop pin", part
L4 952. That is what it is.

**The nipple is the maker's, it is for oil, and it is BSF.** The side
elevation labels the fitting over the back bearing "¼" B.S.F. oil nipple".
Andrew had measured 6.3 mm across and counted about 26 threads to the inch,
which left two candidates of the same size and pitch, BSF and the cycle
thread. The drawing settles it. And it is drawn as an oil nipple, on a
headstock whose plate says use oil only. Whoever packed the pocket under it
with grease was not following the maker, though the ball races beneath
survived it: they show no lift on a 0.01 mm indicator.

**The plug over the front bearing has a number.** The same elevation shows a
knurled plug on a short stem in the hole over the front bearing, part L4 934,
as the catalogue's cutaway does. No size is given, so the three missing plugs
will be made to fit their holes.

**The back bearing is a pair of ball races.** The section shows a plain bush
at the front, two threaded parts in front of it at the nose (L4 905 and
L4 1936), and at the back a pair of angular-contact ball races marked "N.1906
135 A.C. E.P. type". The stepped pulley runs on two ball races of its own,
marked XLS 2" and XLS 2¼". Where the PDH differs from the other two the
drawing says so: it gives the PDH its own spindle, L4/4631 in place of
L4/1938.

**The closer works through a tube.** The PDH sheet shows a tube, L4 4632,
running the length of the spindle bore from the closer at the back to the
collet at the nose. That is how a lever at one end pushes a dead-length
collet at the other. The cap at the nose is L4 763, and Smart & Brown made a
pin spanner for it, L4 2808, drawn as a plain bar with two pins. One will be
made here before the cap is next moved. An inset shows a detail "used up to
& including July prod. 55", and an older toggle closer is drawn and marked
obsolete, so the closer changed more than once.

## One contradiction

The [catalogue](https://www.lathes.co.uk/smart-and-brown-model-L) gives the
PLH and PRH two adjustable bronze bearings, and Andrew, comparing the spare
PRH with its cutaway, found that everything he could see agreed. The parts
book draws the ball races at the back as common to the PLH, PRH and PDH.
Either the PRH changed during its life, or one of the two documents is wrong
about it. Neither says anything about this lathe's own headstock, which is a
PDH and has ball races at the back by Andrew's own eyes. The spare is the only
thing here that can settle what a PRH has, and it goes on the list for when
the spare is looked at.

## What it does not say

- **How oil reaches the front journal.** The section shows a cored space round
  the outside of the bush, which is the space Andrew filled. At the scale of
  these drawings, in a copy of a copy, I can make out no hole or wick through
  the bush. That is not the same as there being none.
- **Which ring at the nose sets the bearing and which locks it.** Both are
  drawn; neither is explained.
- **How much oil, how often,** or anything beyond what the plate already says.
- **Anything about a small fitting on top of the closer's yoke,** part XF 5B,
  where the yoke rides on a ring of balls round the sleeve. It looks like
  another oil point, one that has not been on any list of mine. Whether it is
  one is a question for the lathe.

Those were the questions I had said the book would answer. It answered none
of the ones that stop the spindle running.

## Where that leaves it

The spindle does not turn under power until oil has been seen to reach the
front bearing, and the book was my way of finding out how it is meant to get
there without taking the spindle out. That way is closed. I meant to ask the
people who still service these lathes next: Bracehand had answered my last
letter the morning after I sent it, and their title page asks for exactly
what I now had to give them, the page and the indicating number. The next
morning Andrew ruled that out. In his view the Bracehand route had been
exhausted, and we should go on without it. So the answer has to come from the
lathe: the space round the bush emptied and the top of the bush looked at
before any more oil goes in, and the nose photographed with its cap off to
see which part holds the bush and which locks it. The
[plan](../../reference/plan.md) has the steps.

Meanwhile the session at the stand goes ahead as planned, since none of it
touches the headstock. What the book settled is now on the
[condition register](../../reference/condition.md) and the
[identification page](../../reference/identification.md).
