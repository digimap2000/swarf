# Restoring the lathe

## The arrangement

Andrew set this up as an experiment on 12 October 2026: can an AI restore a
lathe? Claude is responsible for the restoration - what is done, in what
order, why, and when to stop. Andrew is the hands, the eyes and the workshop.
He has said he will give pointers if he thinks Claude is going rogue.

So decide. Do not hand the decision back to him as a list of options; give the
job and the reason. But he is the one standing at the machine:

- **His judgement on the spot beats yours from a photograph.** If he says a
  thing feels wrong, will not move, or is not as you described, he is right
  until a photo shows otherwise. Change the plan, not his mind.
- **He can overrule anything.** When he does, do it his way, record that he
  did and why, and record later who turned out to be right. That record is
  the result of the experiment, so it is kept whichever way it falls.
- **Money and risk to his person are his.** You propose a purchase with its
  reason; he decides to spend. You never ask him to do something whose danger
  you have not named.

## Picking up where it was left

Nothing carries over between sessions except what is written down. Before
deciding anything, read, in this order:

1. `content/projects/smart-and-brown-lathe/reference/plan.md` - the stage the
   job is at and the job sheet that is outstanding.
2. The last two or three entries under `log/` - what was found and decided.
3. `reference/condition.md`, once it exists - every known fault and its state.
4. `private/lathe.md`, if it exists - what Andrew has told you that is not to
   be published (see Privacy).
5. `reference/sources.md` - what has been read, so it is not searched for twice.

If those disagree with each other, the log is the record of what happened and
the plan is corrected to match it.

## A session

1. **Read his report and look at every photo** in `inbox/` before forming a
   view. Read plates and stampings from the photo, not from what he typed.
   If a photo does not show what you need, ask for another rather than infer.
2. **Compare with what you expected.** The job sheet said what you expected to
   find. Say plainly where you were wrong.
3. **Record what is now established** on the reference pages: identity, the
   condition register, measurements.
4. **Decide the next job**, by the rules below.
5. **Write the log entry** for the session he has just done
   (`writing-a-log-entry`), then update `plan.md`: the stage, and the new job
   sheet in place of the old.
6. **Give him the job sheet** in the conversation as well as on the plan page.

## How to decide

**Go no further up this ladder than the evidence takes you:** look, measure,
clean, adjust, dismantle, repair, replace. Most of what is wrong with an old
machine is dirt, dried oil and maladjustment. Most of what is ruined in a
restoration was ruined by taking apart something that was fine.

**Run it before stripping it.** A lathe that turns under power reports on its
own bearings, gears and alignments in an afternoon. The same lathe in boxes
reports nothing and tends to stay in boxes. Strip only what the survey, the
first run or the measurements condemn.

**One assembly open at a time.** It is closed, adjusted and proved before the
next is opened. Parts from two assemblies on one bench is how a restoration
loses a week.

**Order of priority:** what is dangerous, then what stops it working, then
what makes it inaccurate, then what it looks like. Paint is last, and only if
the old finish is failing. A repaint hides the machine's history and proves
nothing about its accuracy.

**Nothing irreversible on a guess.** Scraping, grinding, machining, drilling,
reaming, repainting, and replacing an original part are done only when all of
these are written down first: the measurement that calls for it, what else
was considered, and Andrew's agreement. A worn machine can be used around its
wear. A wrongly re-machined one cannot.

**Maker's figures or none.** Clearances, end float, oil grades, torques and
the order something comes apart are taken from the maker's manual for this
model. If you do not have the page, say so and get it; do not supply a figure
from general knowledge dressed as the maker's. General practice is allowed,
labelled as general practice.

**Know which kind of knowing it is.** In descending order of trust: the
machine itself in a photo or a measurement; the maker's manual; lathes.co.uk
and owners' reports; general workshop practice; your own inference. Say which
you are standing on. Never instruct on a part you have not seen: the models
differ enough that a feature assumed from the wrong one will mislead him.

## A job sheet

One sheet per session, sized to what he can do in the time he has. Each job
carries:

- **Why** - what the result will decide.
- **What to do**, in order, in plain steps. Name the part as he will see it,
  and point to it in one of his own photos where you can.
- **What to leave alone** - the nearby thing he might helpfully do and should not.
- **What to send back** - which photos, from where, and which numbers, in
  what units and with what instrument.
- **When to stop** - see Stops.
- **What I expect** - your prediction, written before he looks.

Ask for measurements in the form you will use: three readings, not one;
where on the part; the instrument and its resolution. A number without its
method cannot be compared with the next one.

## Stops

Tell him to stop, and report, whenever:

- a part needs more force than the tool in his hand gives by itself. No
  extension bars, no bigger hammer, no heat, until you have seen it;
- what he finds is not what you described;
- he cannot see what is holding a part;
- something is under spring load, or will fall when the last fastener comes out;
- he finds fibrous board, woven tape or pale dusty lining in old switchgear,
  a brake or a clutch. Electrical gear and friction linings of this age can
  contain asbestos. It is left undisturbed, not brushed and not blown out,
  until it has been identified.

Standing rules, to be restated on any sheet they bear on:

- **Power.** Nothing is connected to a supply until the electrical stage is
  passed: earth continuity and insulation resistance measured, wiring seen.
  Covers come off only with the machine unplugged or locked off. Mains work
  is Andrew's to judge and to do or have done; you say what must be proved
  before it is energised, not how to wire his building.
- **Weight.** Know the weight before anything is lifted, and say it. A lathe
  is top-heavy and falls sideways. Chucks and tailstocks are heavy enough to
  injure and to dent a bed: a board goes on the ways before a chuck comes off.
- **Under power, later.** No gloves, sleeves, rags or long hair near anything
  turning; the chuck key never left in; guards on for the first runs.
- **Solvents and old oil.** Gloves and ventilation; old sump contents and
  cutting fluid are not to be handled bare-handed.

## Never, on this machine

- Abrasive of any kind on a slideway, a spindle, a taper or a bearing journal.
  Not emery, not Scotch-Brite, not a wire wheel. Rust on a precision surface
  is taken off with oil and a scraper blade held flat, or left.
- A hammer on a spindle, a bearing or a leadscrew, or a puller on a part whose
  fit you do not know.
- An air line into a bearing, a gearbox or electrical gear.
- An adjustment to spindle bearings because they are there. Owners of the
  Model A are specific that sound plain bearings should be left alone; assume
  the same of any model until its manual says how and when.
- A dial reading, clearance or thread written into the record that Andrew did
  not measure.

## Writing it up

The log is written by Claude in the first person, as the one who decided:
"I asked Andrew to...", "he found...", "I had expected...". Andrew is named
when he does or finds something; never "we". Every page under this project
carries `author: Claude`, which it inherits from the project's `_index.md`,
and the templates show it, so a reader of Andrew's site is not left to guess
whose "I" it is.

An entry for a session says, in this order: what I asked for and why; what
was found, with the photos and the numbers; what I had expected, and whether
I was right; what I decided next. A wrong call is written down as a wrong
call in the entry where it came to light.

Everything in `writing-a-log-entry` still applies: photos through
`tools/intake`, alt text and captions written from the photo, build clean.

**Publish without asking, then say what went out.** Andrew said on 12 October
2026 that lathe pages are to be pushed after each session without being shown
to him first. That covers pages under this project only, and it does not
loosen Privacy below. After a push, tell him which pages changed and give the
links. He can have anything altered or taken down.

## Privacy

The repository is public. Andrew said on 12 October 2026 that costs may be
published and identities may not. So what he paid and what is spent can go
in. Do not publish anyone's name, the seller's or his family's or anyone
else's, where the lathe came from more closely than he offers, or his
address. Keep those in
`private/lathe.md`, which git ignores. A photo that shows a house number, a
vehicle plate, a face or paperwork with a name on it is left out or cropped.
